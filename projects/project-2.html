Frequency Capping

Six in-depth interviews with senior media planners — before a single screen was designed — reframed a long-standing feature request and changed which of two technical directions the team pursued.

Context & problem

For a long time, planners had been asking for "frequency capping" — a way to stop the same person from seeing an ad too many times. The obvious response is to build a cap: a maximum number, one setting, one control.

Before committing engineering time to that, we wanted to test a real hypothesis first: were senior planners actually asking for a literal cap, or using "frequency capping" as a proxy for a bigger, less obvious problem?

Process

Instead of a standard discovery guide, I wrote the research around two explicit, falsifiable hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 — The Strategic Imperative: decisions about frequency are made at the planning-strategy stage, not as a tactical afterthought — planners already have a "frequency philosophy" before they ever touch a platform setting.

Hypothesis 2 — The Proxy Problem: when senior planners ask for "frequency capping," they aren't literally asking for a cap button. They're using the term as a stand-in for their real goal — stopping the optimizer from over-saturating one channel and wasting budget.

I interviewed six senior users — from strategic planning directors to highly tactical buyers, across EU and US markets — with structured follow-up probes designed to surface which hypothesis actually held.

What the interviews showed

The interviews were unanimous: a cross-channel, budget-efficiency solution was seen as far more valuable and strategic than a simple platform-specific cap. Four things stood out:

  • The real problem is waste, not capping — described as "contact pollution" or "controlling investment levels," not a cap for its own sake.
  • The unsolved problem is cross-media — even the most digital-focused participant chose the cross-channel option, because real campaigns mix TV, out-of-home, and digital.
  • A single-platform cap would have solved a problem several users already considered minor or handled elsewhere.
  • "Frequency capping" was consistently shorthand for a bigger, unsolved budget-efficiency problem.
TL;DR summary & key findings

Impact

No feature shipped yet — but the direction did. What changed:

  • The team committed to the right build before starting it. Six interviews settled which of two technical directions to pursue — the cross-channel, budget-efficiency one — at the discovery stage, not mid-sprint.
  • A long-standing request got decoded. "Frequency capping" turned out to be shorthand for a bigger, unsolved efficiency problem. Naming the real goal changed what went on the roadmap.
