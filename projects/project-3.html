Express Scenarios

A 0→1 tool I led on research and design — and let the studies keep proving us wrong.

Four years on one product. I planned and led the research end to end — discovery, a concept test, and three rounds on the live tool, one of them a year after launch that turned a shipped design around. This is how I worked with the PO and the business, not a feature tour.

Role
Research & design lead
→ design support

2022 – now

WPP Media

The problem

Planners build media plans in Campaigns — the heavier planning app, built for year-long strategies and large budgets, and excellent at that. In a two-hour workshop with planners from markets around the world, one thing was clear: for a short, tactical campaign it is too heavy, with a high barrier to entry.

We started with two bets. One: a lighter tool would let a junior planner plan without the full machinery — persona work had given us three planner types who use our tools daily, plus a fourth, a junior in a media house. Two: the same simplicity would give experienced planners a fast way to handle short campaigns and answer a client’s budget “what if” on the spot.

The first bet was wrong, and it took the research a year to prove it — more on that below. The second one carried the product.

“The simple setup is a relief — I get to a plan without the spreadsheet marathon.” — planner, research session
“Some days I’m on 150 calls. I used to have help with the decks — now it’s all me.” — media planner, interview

On Express, research wasn’t a phase bolted onto the product — it ran continuously, with the PO in the room, so every call weighed the business case against what planners actually needed. Several passes later, V3 covers most of the pain points those sessions surfaced.

The design bet: reuse the algorithm we already had in the heavier tool, and simplify everything around it — strip the setup to the few things a planner has to choose, and show only what they read on the results.

2022 · designing the first version

Nine steps in the heavy tool. One screen in V1.

The setup was the whole point — and where my first assumption showed up in the design. It went through wireframes and a vote before a pixel was committed.

First, wireframes in front of planners.

I sketched the setup and a multi-scenario results screen at low fidelity and took them straight into research — cheap enough to throw away, concrete enough for a planner to react to.

Then three layouts, and a vote.

The direction became three candidate layouts for the results screen. In a concept-test survey in August 2022, planners reacted to each one on its own — in writing — before any overall vote.

The votes tied. I built neither option.

Two layouts drew equal votes — but the written feedback pointed somewhere neither had gone: people wanted one option’s stacked, expandable scenario cards with another’s detailed per-scenario breakdown.

So I read the survey for what people were asking for, not what they picked, and built the combination none of the three had. Reacting to each option in isolation — before any vote — was deliberate: it kept a fixable complaint about one layout from reading as a preference for another.

2023 · V1 ships

One screen. Only the choices a planner has to make.

The heavier tool walks through nine steps to start a campaign. V1 of Express brought it to a single Details screen: name, budget, dates, audience — everything else on smart defaults. Same algorithm as the heavier tool; a setup stripped to the essentials around it. Few enough decisions for the junior planner we thought we were building for.

January 2024 · a year after V1 shipped

A year in, I ran the round that turned it around.

Eight in-depth interviews — planners who used Express, and some who didn’t. Two findings reshaped the design. A third reshaped who it was for.

“Four scenarios are interesting — but when I plan, I know my goal. It’s predetermined.” — media planner, interview

Finding one

Four goal-scenarios were a curiosity, not a decision aid.

V1 handed every planner four scenarios, each optimised for a different campaign goal — no goal to pick. But planners always know their goal before they start. So V2: you choose your goal in setup (that’s scenario one), and compare it against one always optimised for maximum reach (scenario two).

“I use it to test an idea and iterate fast if the client asks for a change — in a quick, convenient way.” — senior media planner, satisfaction survey

Finding two

What clients actually kept asking was budget.

“What happens if we spend 10% more, or less?” came up constantly — and answering it meant rebuilding the campaign, re-running the simulation, and waiting. So instead of four goal scenarios, V2 computes four budget variants together: −20%, −10%, +10% and +20% of the base budget.

It also changed how the plan gets presented: planners started putting the budget-variant view and the reach curve straight into the client deck — a way to show “we know the budget can move, and we’re ready for it.”

Finding three · who it was for

The junior we built it for wasn’t there — and I own the miss.

In our agencies, juniors don’t plan campaigns solo — so the planner we’d stripped the setup for was never going to show up. The persona was real; I hadn’t pinned down the working context around it before we committed. What saved the product: the same simplicity was exactly what experienced planners wanted for short campaigns and fast client turnarounds.

V2 · the setup, rebuilt

More control, and still one screen.

What the findings changed about the setup itself.

The setup

A nine-step wizard, collapsed onto one screen.

V2 gave planners the controls V1 never had — the campaign goal chosen up front, which touchpoints reach each audience, and the effective frequency set by hand. Everything else stays on smart defaults, settings are grouped so each sits with its context — and it is still one page.

How I ran the research

I planned and led it end to end — and I never run a session alone. Most interviews were with the PO, so we heard the business need under the user need, and always with planners from different markets. Over four years that came to around 40 conversations — 27 of them in-depth interviews I ran myself, across three post-launch rounds (January 2024, March 2024, January 2025), plus a layout-preference survey in 2022 and a satisfaction survey with 82 responses in January 2025.

With the PO I analysed each round and we prioritised together what to build, and in what order. My job was to make sure every call had evidence behind it — and to notice when the evidence had changed.

2023–24 · measuring success

The team’s first success metrics — and what they couldn’t tell us.

After a conference I pushed for the team to attend, we built our first proper success-metrics framework for Express — leading and lagging indicators, agreed up front rather than reverse-engineered later.

Then it hit a wall: adoption was too low to read a clean UX signal. You can’t separate a design problem from an exposure problem when barely anyone is in the tool. That gap is what led to the experiment below.

Late 2024 · the experiment

Loved in every session, and still barely used.

In usability sessions Express scored highly — planners liked it. But the user base stayed low, and that gap puzzled us. Running more sessions wasn’t going to explain it.

So instead of booking more sessions, I designed an experiment. The question: is low adoption a problem with the tool — UX friction, weak onboarding — or with the environment around it, where the company doesn’t ask planners to switch and gives them no time to learn a new tool? We ran a proper training for one team and set up a chat to stay in constant contact as they used it.

The result confirmed the hypothesis. The tool is intuitive; a few parameters on the results screen needed explaining; but adoption in that team still stayed low. Planners work in Excel and the tools they already trust — and without a reason from management to switch, nothing moved.

A while later — a year on — the user base grew sharply. Not from a redesign: Polish management had set increasing use of our apps as an employee goal and was tracking usage levels across the team.

Lesson

UX and UI are a major driver of adoption — but not the only one. Sometimes the bigger factor is the users’ environment: what the organisation rewards, and which tools people already trust. Good design doesn’t override that on its own.

January 2025 · the handoff

Reading the data straight

When I handed the product on, the colleague taking over and I went through the usage data together — analytics events, overlap with the heavier tool, how often people came back. My job in that room was to keep us honest: a spike in first-time use traced back to a pop-up we’d run in the campaign module, not to real demand — and it would have flattered every conclusion after it.

The read that held up: 63% of Express users never build a scenario in the heavier tool. For a big share of them, Express is the whole job — not a warm-up.

The signals we kept

Every unmet request from the interviews and the survey went on one triaged board — should do vs. needs more observation. The biggest Express complaints — editing and duplicating scenarios, a per-audience cost table, loading speed — were cleared in V3.

The survey, unspun

82 planners answered in January 2025. Three charts I keep coming back to.

V3 — and a handoff on purpose

The iteration the January 2025 survey briefed. A colleague leads it; I moved to support.

V3

What the survey asked for, built.

The January 2025 responses were specific: planners wanted to add their own scenarios, and to edit the per-touchpoint investment the tool proposes instead of taking it as given. V3 does both — and clears the biggest items off the signal board.

A colleague took design lead here; I moved to support, unblocking and regular design reviews — and to a data-heavy application where deep business and industry knowledge mattered. Handing on a mature product with room to grow was the right call, for it and for me.

From early 2026 · my first AI project

Now: an AI layer, and a new set of things to learn

Running alongside V3: scenarios created and edited by talking to an agent in a chat panel. A colleague leads the design; I support and unblock the hard parts — and it’s my first project designing an AI interaction. What I’m building up: the current patterns for AI and chat interfaces, what the technology actually requires, how a model “thinks” — and the guardrails a real planning workflow needs around an LLM.

What the work moved

The budget answer moved into the client conversation.

What used to be a post-meeting rebuild is now a live view planners put straight in the client deck — a way to show the budget can move and they’re ready for it.

A shipped design, reversed by its own research.

V1’s four-scenario model went live, then the post-launch round replaced it. The willingness to unship is the point.

Less rework at the expensive end.

Seniors prototype a direction in Express first, then commit it in the heavy tool with full control.

A measurement habit the team kept.

The success-metrics framework I built here has been reused on later work.
