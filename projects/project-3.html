Express Scenarios

A media-planning tool I’ve shaped from the first concept — through the research rounds that kept redirecting it.

I’m the research and design lead on Express Scenarios, a light tool for planning short campaigns. Over four years I ran the discovery, designed V1 and V2, and led the studies that reset the brief each time — working closely with the Product Owner and the business as a partner in the calls, not just a supplier of findings. This page is the methods and the decisions behind the versions, not a feature tour.

“Building all the budget scenarios by hand used to take real time. Here they’re ready — so I get back to the client faster. I’m a big fan.” — senior media planner, in-depth interview

The product

Three versions, each one the research’s idea.

Planners build media plans in Campaigns — the heavier app, built for year-long strategies and large budgets. A two-hour workshop with planners worldwide made the gap clear: for a short, tactical campaign it’s too heavy. Express is the light path — here’s how it changed, and why.

V1 · 2023

One screen. Only the choices a planner has to make.

The heavier tool walks through nine steps to start a campaign. V1 brought it to a single Details screen — name, budget, dates, audience — everything else on smart defaults, the same algorithm underneath. It shipped with four goal-scenarios, one per objective, and no goal to pick.

My part: discovery, the wireframes, a concept test on three results layouts, and the V1 design.

“Four scenarios are interesting — but when I plan, I know my goal. It’s predetermined.” — media planner, interview

V2 · 2024

Two scenarios, and budget variants.

Eight in-depth interviews a year after launch reset the design. Planners always know their goal — so four goal-scenarios became two: your goal, versus maximum reach. And what clients kept asking was budget, so V2 computes four budget variants together — −20, −10, +10 and +20% of the base. Planners started putting the budget-variant view straight into the client deck.

My part: the eight interviews, the analysis with the PO, and the V2 design — setup and results.

V2 · the setup, rebuilt

More control, and still one screen.

The setup

A nine-step wizard, collapsed onto one screen.

V2 gave planners the controls V1 never had — the campaign goal chosen up front, which touchpoints reach each audience, and the effective frequency set by hand. Everything else stays on smart defaults, settings are grouped so each sits with its context — and it is still one page.

V3 · 2025 →

Scenarios planners build, and investment they can edit.

The January 2025 survey was specific: planners wanted to add their own scenarios and to edit the per-touchpoint investment the tool proposes instead of taking it as given. V3 does both — and clears the biggest items off the signal board (editing and duplicating scenarios, a per-audience cost table, loading speed).

My part: the research round that set the brief, then design reviews and support — a colleague took design lead on V3.

How I worked

How I made the calls — and checked them.

The methods behind the versions — a concept test, a metrics framework, an adoption experiment, an honest read of the data. Not in date order.

Concept test · 2022

The votes tied. I built neither option.

Planners saw three candidate layouts for the results screen and reacted to each one in writing, on its own, before any vote. Two drew equal votes — but the written feedback pointed somewhere neither had gone: people wanted one option’s stacked, expandable scenario cards with another’s detailed per-scenario breakdown.

So I read the survey for what people were asking for, not what they picked, and built the combination none of the three had. Reacting in isolation first was deliberate — it kept a fixable complaint about one layout from reading as a preference for another.

Metrics, agreed up front

A Goal / Question / Metric framework, before the numbers.

After a conference I pushed for us to attend, I ran a workshop to build Express’s first proper success-metrics framework — goals, the questions under each, and candidate metrics, voted on together. Leading and lagging indicators agreed up front, not reverse-engineered later.

Then it hit a wall: adoption was too low to read a clean UX signal. You can’t separate a design problem from an exposure problem when barely anyone is in the tool — which is what led to the experiment.

Late 2024 · the experiment

Loved in every session, and still barely used.

In usability sessions Express scored highly — planners liked it. But the user base stayed low, and that gap puzzled us. Running more sessions wasn’t going to explain it.

So instead of booking more sessions, I designed an experiment. The question: is low adoption a problem with the tool — UX friction, weak onboarding — or with the environment around it, where the company doesn’t ask planners to switch and gives them no time to learn a new tool? We ran a proper training for one team and set up a chat to stay in constant contact as they used it.

The result confirmed the hypothesis. The tool is intuitive; a few parameters on the results screen needed explaining; but adoption in that team still stayed low. Planners work in Excel and the tools they already trust — and without a reason from management to switch, nothing moved.

A while later — a year on — the user base grew sharply. Not from a redesign: Polish management had set increasing use of our apps as an employee goal and was tracking usage levels across the team.

Lesson

UX and UI are a major driver of adoption — but not the only one. Sometimes the bigger factor is the users’ environment: what the organisation rewards, and which tools people already trust. Good design doesn’t override that on its own.

January 2025 · the handoff

Reading the data straight

When I handed the product on, the colleague taking over and I went through the usage data together — analytics events, overlap with the heavier tool, how often people came back. My job in that room was to keep us honest: a spike in first-time use traced back to a pop-up we’d run in the campaign module, not to real demand — and it would have flattered every conclusion after it.

The read that held up: 63% of Express users never build a scenario in the heavier tool. For a big share of them, Express is the whole job — not a warm-up.

“The tool is very fluid, very simple, very user-friendly. What could be improved is the output.” — senior media planner, interview

The signals we kept

Every unmet request from the interviews and the survey went on one triaged board — should do vs. needs more observation. The biggest Express complaints — editing and duplicating scenarios, a per-audience cost table, loading speed — were cleared in V3.

What the evidence changed

What the research overturned — and what it didn’t move.

V1’s four-scenario model, unshipped by its own research.

It went live, then the post-launch round replaced it. Planners always know their goal, so four goal-scenarios were a curiosity, not a decision aid.

The junior we designed for wasn’t a real user.

In our agencies juniors don’t plan solo. The persona was real; I hadn’t pinned down the working context around it before we committed. What saved the product: experienced planners wanted the same simplicity for short campaigns.

Adoption didn’t move on UX alone.

Loved in every session, still barely used. It moved a year later — when management made “use our apps” an employee goal and tracked it.

Speed was the value, not the scenarios.

The survey put fast calculation and fast setup ahead of the budget-variant and multi-scenario features we’d bet the redesign on.

V3 · the results screen

V3 — and a handoff on purpose.

V3

What the survey asked for, built.

The January 2025 responses were specific: planners wanted to add their own scenarios, and to edit the per-touchpoint investment the tool proposes instead of taking it as given. V3 does both — and clears the biggest items off the signal board.

A colleague took design lead here; I moved to support, unblocking and regular design reviews — and to a data-heavy application where deep business and industry knowledge mattered. Handing on a mature product with room to grow was the right call, for it and for me.

From early 2026 · my first AI project

Now: an AI layer, and a new set of things to learn

Running alongside V3: scenarios created and edited by talking to an agent in a chat panel. A colleague leads the design; I support and unblock the hard parts — and it’s my first project designing an AI interaction. What I’m building up: the current patterns for AI and chat interfaces, what the technology actually requires, how a model “thinks” — and the guardrails a real planning workflow needs around an LLM.

What the work moved

The budget answer moved into the client conversation.

What used to be a post-meeting rebuild is now a live view planners put straight in the client deck — a way to show the budget can move and they’re ready for it.
