Express Scenarios

A stripped-down way to build and compare media plans — built from zero in 2022, then reshaped over four years by research that kept overturning what we assumed. Today it's growing an AI layer.

Role
Research & design lead
→ design support
Team
Product Owner, Product Designers, Data Science, Engineering
Timeframe
2022 – now
(4 years, ongoing)
Company
WPP Media

Context & problem

Media planners at our agencies build their plans in Campaigns — another app in our family of tools. It's built for year-long strategies and campaigns with very large budgets, and it's excellent at that. But planning a short, tactical campaign there — four weeks, a smaller budget — takes a lot of time. Planners always have a budget to work to; what slows them down is that clients like to explore variants and ask "what if we shifted the split, what if we spent more here?"

The team's bet: a much simpler tool on top of the same planning engine — a stripped-down setup, only the essentials on the results — so a planner could build and compare scenarios for a shorter, smaller campaign fast, and answer a client's "what if" on the spot. I led research and design on it from discovery through the first big redesign; from the 2025 iteration onward a colleague took design lead and I moved to support.

Process

Nearly four years, many research rounds, and a few assumptions that didn't survive contact with users. Step by step:

1. Discovery — a day in a planner's life

Persona work surfaced three real user types — plus a junior planner we defined but couldn't actually find among our users. A two-hour focus group with eight planners across the world mapped their day: pain points, tools, and which tasks cost them the most. I planned and ran the study, and owned the synthesis — Conclusions, Goals, Pain points, How can we help.

2. A bet on simplicity

In a team workshop we shaped the idea: reuse the existing algorithm and data model, strip the setup right down, and drop the advanced parameters newer planners don't read. I mapped user flows for the features coming out of brainstorming, defined what must live on a scenario card — the "ingredients" — and ranked each element by priority, then designed four layout directions.

3. Concept testing by survey

The team voted the four directions down to three. I built a structured survey: react to each option on its own — likes, pains, ideas — before voting overall, so a fixable complaint about one layout wouldn't get read as a preference for another. Then I extracted the insights and wrote up the conclusions.

The votes tied between two options — but the writing pointed somewhere else. People wanted one option's stacked, expandable scenario cards combined with another's detailed per-scenario breakdown. We built the combination none of the three had on its own.

4. Reality check → redesign

New research pushed back on the core idea. Planners told us they always have one campaign goal — and clients often just want maximum reach — so showing four goal-based scenarios didn't match how they work. We redesigned the setup and the results around two scenarios: one impact-focused option the planner picks in setup, and one always optimised for reach. We added a way to show budget variants — "what if we spend 15% more, or less?" — a question clients asked constantly.

5. Maturity & handover (2025)

A year after the redesign shipped, research said people were happy — but wanted to edit the algorithm's budget split by hand, duplicate a scenario, and tweak it inside one campaign. A colleague took design lead on this iteration; I moved to support and unblocking.

6. An agentic layer (2026 – now)

The current chapter: creating and editing scenarios by talking to an Agent in a chat panel. My colleague leads; I support the design and help unblock the harder problems — and it's where I've built hands-on understanding of what LLMs do well, where they fail confidently, and the guardrails a product has to put around them.

Impact

What changed for the business — not just the screen.

  • Client "what if" questions get answered in the room. Budget-variant scenarios that used to mean rebuilding the plan in Campaigns after the meeting now take minutes, live in the pitch.
  • A shorter ramp onto the planning methodology. Express is where planners learn the approach; in markets that trained their teams, use of it rose sharply.
  • Less rework at the expensive end. Senior planners prototype a direction in Express first, then commit it in Campaigns with full control — fewer wasted cycles in the heavy tool.
  • A measurement habit the team kept. The project produced the team's first success-metrics framework, now reused on later work.

Measuring success

This was the team's first serious attempt at metrics — set up after a conference I brought back to them. In our first metrics workshop we defined what to track: time to a finished plan, repeat use, and qualitative satisfaction.

Then adoption undercut the signal. Too few planners were using Express to read anything reliable about the UX itself. Rather than guess, in 2025 I designed an experiment to find out why — was it the product, or something around it?

The cause was systemic, not the design. Planners aren't given time to learn new tools, and the company doesn't carve that time out. Where formal training did happen — the Polish market ran sessions — Express usage rose sharply. That pointed at time-to-learn as the real blocker, not the interface.

What I won't claim: I can show the usage increase in analytics, but it tracks the training rollout, not a design change — so it isn't a UX win and I don't present it as one. What the work produced instead was an evidenced reason for the gap, and a case for training rather than another redesign.

Voice of users

A short readout from research sessions across the years — what worked, what to watch, what confused people, and what they asked for.

Good

  • "The simple setup is a relief — I get to a plan without a spreadsheet marathon."
  • "Less on the screen means I actually read it."
  • "Good for testing an idea before I build the real thing in Campaigns."

Watch outs

  • "Four scenarios for different goals? I always know my goal before I start."
  • "Half the time the client just wants maximum reach — that's the whole brief."
  • "I don't have time to sit down and learn another tool."

Errors

  • "I couldn't tell which scenario was the one I'd chosen and which was the reach one."
  • "I expected the algorithm's numbers to be editable — and couldn't change them."

Learnings

✓

Four years of research rounds meant assumptions got caught and corrected — not shipped and quietly forgotten.

✓

I brought the team its first metrics practice, and treated weak adoption as something to investigate, not hide.

✓

I handed design lead to a colleague when it was time, and shifted to support — the work was better for it.

Next time

We should have tested the "for juniors" idea against how agencies actually let juniors work — much earlier.

Next time

The first version over-modelled the problem with four goal-scenarios. The simpler two-scenario version could have shipped sooner and taught us the same thing faster.

Next time

Measurement is the craft I most want to go deeper on — designing for signal from the start, not retrofitting it once adoption is already thin.
