Express Scenarios

A 0→1 tool I led on research and design — and let the studies keep proving us wrong.

Four years on one product. I planned and led the research end to end — discovery, a concept test, and a round on the live tool a year after launch that turned a shipped design around. This is how I worked with the PO and the business, not a feature tour.

The problem

Planners build campaigns in Campaigns — a tool built for year-long strategies and large budgets, and excellent at that. In a two-hour workshop with planners from markets around the world, one thing was clear: for a short, tactical campaign the tool is too heavy, with a high barrier to entry.

Persona work set the frame. Three planner types who use our tools day to day — and a fourth, a junior planner, who works in media houses but doesn’t use these apps at all. So this wasn’t about training wheels for beginners; it was about speed for experienced planners.

The problem I was designing for: let an experienced planner build and compare scenarios for a short campaign fast, and answer a client’s budget “what if” live — without carrying the weight of the full tool.

“The simple setup is a relief — I get to a plan without the spreadsheet marathon.” — planner, research session

Much of that discovery I ran with the PO — on purpose, so we heard the business need under the user need. With that, I argued for the smallest version that could still carry a real client conversation.

Strip the setup. Keep only what a planner reads on the results. Reuse the algorithm the org already trusted.

The setup

A nine-step wizard, collapsed onto one screen.

The full tool walks through nine steps to start a campaign. Express brings it down to a single-page form: smart defaults on every field the data could justify, settings grouped so each sits with its context, and controls v1 never had — planners now pick which touchpoints reach each audience, and set the effective frequency themselves.

~2024 · a year after v1 shipped

A year in, I ran the round that turned it around.

Nine in-depth interviews — planners who used Express, and some who didn’t. Two findings reshaped the product.

Finding one

Four goal-scenarios were a curiosity, not a decision aid.

V1 handed every planner four scenarios, each optimised for a different campaign goal — no goal to pick. But planners always know their goal before they start. So v2: you choose your goal in setup (that’s scenario one), and compare it against one always optimised for maximum reach (scenario two).

“I use it to test an idea and iterate quickly if the client asks for a change. Express Scenarios lets me do it in a fast, convenient way.” — senior media planner, satisfaction survey (Q2 2025)

Finding two

What clients actually kept asking was budget.

“What happens if we spend 10% more, or less?” came up constantly — and answering it meant rebuilding the campaign, re-running the simulation, and waiting. So instead of four goal scenarios, v2 computes four budget variants together: −20%, −10%, +10% and +20% of the base budget.

How I ran the research

I planned and led it end to end — and I never run a session alone. Most interviews were with the PO, so we heard the business need under the user need, and always with planners from different markets. Over four years that meant roughly 40 in-depth interviews, a layout-preference survey in 2022, and a satisfaction survey in Q2 2025 with 88 responses.

With the PO I analysed each round and we prioritised together what to build, and in what order. My job was to make sure every call had evidence behind it — and to notice when the evidence had changed.

2026 · AI layer

My first AI project

Scenarios by conversation. I’m learning the current patterns for AI/chat interaction, what the tech needs, and how the model actually “thinks” — and the guardrails a real workflow needs.

The votes tied. I built neither option.

In the concept test, two layouts drew equal votes. But the written feedback pointed somewhere neither had gone: people wanted one option’s stacked, expandable scenario cards with another’s detailed per-scenario breakdown.

So I read the survey for what people were asking for, not what they picked, and built the combination none of the three had on its own. Reacting to each option in isolation — before any vote — was a deliberate part of the survey design: it kept a fixable complaint about one layout from reading as a preference for another.

The experiment

Loved in every session, and still barely used.

In usability sessions Express scored highly — planners liked it. But the user base stayed low, and that gap puzzled us. Running more sessions wasn’t going to explain it.

So with the Product Owner I designed an experiment. The question: is low adoption a problem with the tool — UX friction, weak onboarding — or with the environment around it, where the company doesn’t ask planners to switch and gives them no time to learn a new tool? We ran a proper training for one team and set up a chat to stay in constant contact as they used it.

The result confirmed the hypothesis. The tool is intuitive; a few parameters on the results screen needed explaining; but adoption in that team still stayed low. Planners work in Excel and the tools they already trust — and without a reason from management to switch, nothing moved.

A while later the Polish market jumped ~50% year on year. Not from a redesign — management had run trainings and made “use our apps more” a 2025 performance goal.

Lesson

UX and UI are a major driver of adoption — but not the only one. Sometimes the bigger factor is the users’ environment: what the organisation rewards, and which tools people already trust. Good design doesn’t override that on its own.

Express V3 — and a handoff on purpose

The iteration after the 2025 survey. Then I passed it on.

V3

What the survey asked for, built.

The Q2 2025 responses were specific: planners wanted to add their own scenarios, and to edit the per-touchpoint investment the tool proposes instead of taking it as given. V3 does both.

Then I handed the project to a colleague on the team — a mature product with room to grow is a good thing to pass on — and moved to a data-heavy application where deep business and industry knowledge mattered, which is what I brought.

2026 — now · my first AI project

Now: an AI layer, and a new set of things to learn

Scenarios created and edited by talking to an agent in a chat panel. A colleague leads the design; I support and unblock the hard parts — and it’s my first project designing an AI interaction. What I’m building up: the current patterns for AI and chat interfaces, what the technology actually requires, how a model “thinks” — and the guardrails a real planning workflow needs around an LLM.

What the work moved

Client “what if” gets answered in the room.

Budget-variant scenarios that meant a post-meeting rebuild now take minutes, live in the pitch.

A shipped design, reversed by its own research.

V1’s four-scenario model went live, then the post-launch round replaced it. The willingness to unship is the point.

Less rework at the expensive end.

Seniors prototype a direction in Express first, then commit it in the heavy tool with full control.

A measurement habit the team kept.

The success-metrics framework I built here has been reused on later work.
